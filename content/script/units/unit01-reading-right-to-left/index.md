## 1. Objectives

By the end of this unit you can:

- follow an Arabic line with your eye in the direction it is actually written;
- name and write the five letters {{< ar "ب ت ث ن ي" >}} and tell them apart by
  their dots alone;
- recognise each of those letters in the four shapes it takes according to its
  place in the word;
- read the three short-vowel marks — {{< ar "ـَ" >}} *fatḥa*, {{< ar "ـِ" >}}
  *kasra*, {{< ar "ـُ" >}} *ḍamma* — and say the syllable they make;
- read four fully vocalised Arabic words aloud, correctly, at first sight.

Nothing here asks you to *speak* Arabic yet. This unit is about the eye and the
hand.

## 2. Input

### A. Direction

Arabic runs from right to left. The first letter of a word sits at the
right-hand end of it; the first word of a line sits at the right-hand margin;
the first page of a book is the one you would call the back cover.

Two things do **not** turn round.

- **Numbers run left to right**, in either set of digits. In the address
  {{< ar "شارِع ٣٤" tr="šāriʿ 34" gloss="34 Street" >}} the digits are read
  three-four exactly as you would read them in English, even though the line
  around them runs the other way.
- **Latin words keep their own direction** when they are quoted inside an Arabic
  sentence.

This is not a quirk of a particular font; it is how the writing system works,
and every piece of software that displays Arabic implements the same
bidirectional algorithm to do it. Practically, it means your eye has to change
gear in the middle of a line and change back — which feels effortful for about a
fortnight and then stops being noticeable.

### B. One skeleton, five letters

Arabic letters come in families that share a shape and differ only in their
dots. The largest family is the one below: a single tooth, {{< ar "ـبـ" >}},
with dots placed above or below it.

| Letter | Name | Sound | Isolated | Initial | Medial | Final |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| {{< ar "ب" >}} | bāʾ | *b* as in *bed* | {{< ar "ب" >}} | {{< ar "بـ" >}} | {{< ar "ـبـ" >}} | {{< ar "ـب" >}} |
| {{< ar "ت" >}} | tāʾ | *t* as in *tin* | {{< ar "ت" >}} | {{< ar "تـ" >}} | {{< ar "ـتـ" >}} | {{< ar "ـت" >}} |
| {{< ar "ث" >}} | ṯāʾ | *th* as in *think* | {{< ar "ث" >}} | {{< ar "ثـ" >}} | {{< ar "ـثـ" >}} | {{< ar "ـث" >}} |
| {{< ar "ن" >}} | nūn | *n* as in *net* | {{< ar "ن" >}} | {{< ar "نـ" >}} | {{< ar "ـنـ" >}} | {{< ar "ـن" >}} |
| {{< ar "ي" >}} | yāʾ | *y* as in *yes* | {{< ar "ي" >}} | {{< ar "يـ" >}} | {{< ar "ـيـ" >}} | {{< ar "ـي" >}} |

The dots are the whole distinction: one below is *bāʾ*, two above *tāʾ*, three
above *ṯāʾ*, one above *nūn*, two below *yāʾ*. Miss a dot and you have written a
different letter — which is why handwriting practice in this stage is
dot-by-dot, not word-by-word.

In the middle of a word all five look nearly identical, a tooth with dots. At
the end of a word *nūn* and *yāʾ* drop into a deep bowl below the line, which is
how you tell them from *bāʾ*, *tāʾ* and *ṯāʾ* at a glance.

{{< callout type="tip" title="A hyphen in a letter table is not punctuation" >}}
When a letter is printed as {{< ar "ـبـ" >}} or {{< ar "بـ" >}}, the little
stroke shows the side on which the letter joins its neighbour — on the right,
on the left, or on both. The stroke is not part of the letter and is never
written in a real word.
{{< /callout >}}

### C. The three short vowels

Arabic writes three short vowels as small marks — *ḥarakāt* — above or below the
consonant they follow. They are not letters, and in ordinary printed Arabic they
are simply left out. Everything in this stage and at A1 is written with them.

| Mark | Name | Value | On *bāʾ* | Read as |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| {{< ar "ـَ" >}} | fatḥa | short *a* | {{< ar "بَ" >}} | *ba* |
| {{< ar "ـِ" >}} | kasra | short *i* | {{< ar "بِ" >}} | *bi* |
| {{< ar "ـُ" >}} | ḍamma | short *u* | {{< ar "بُ" >}} | *bu* |

The mark is read **after** the consonant it sits on, never before. {{< ar "بِ" >}}
is *bi*, never *ib*.

A fourth mark, the *sukūn* {{< ar "ـْ" >}}, means the opposite: this consonant
carries **no** vowel. It appears in two of the words below because Arabic words
end in a bare consonant more often than not. Unit 4 treats it properly; for now,
read a letter marked with {{< ar "ـْ" >}} as a plain consonant with nothing after
it.

### D. First words

Read each word from the right. The transliteration is DIN 31635 and is a
crutch — cover it as soon as you can.

{{< ar-block title="Read aloud" >}}
بَيْت — بِنْت — بَيْن — تِبْن
{{< /ar-block >}}

| Word | Letters, right to left | DIN 31635 | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| {{< ar "بَيْت" >}} | bāʾ + fatḥa · yāʾ + sukūn · tāʾ | *bayt* | house |
| {{< ar "بِنْت" >}} | bāʾ + kasra · nūn + sukūn · tāʾ | *bint* | girl, daughter |
| {{< ar "بَيْن" >}} | bāʾ + fatḥa · yāʾ + sukūn · nūn | *bayn* | between |
| {{< ar "تِبْن" >}} | tāʾ + kasra · bāʾ + sukūn · nūn | *tibn* | straw |

Note what the joining does to {{< ar "بِنْت" >}}: three letters, one continuous
line, and the dots — one below, one above, two above — are the only thing
telling you which three.

### E. In Damascus you would hear…

The course teaches Modern Standard Arabic; the recurring cast lives in Damascus,
and where the Levantine pronunciation differs audibly the difference is flagged
so that the spoken language does not come as a shock.

- {{< ar "بَيْت" >}} is *bayt* in careful MSA. In Damascus the diphthong
  flattens and it is heard as **bēt**, with one long vowel.
- {{< ar "بِنْت" >}} keeps its consonants but the vowel is reduced, closer to
  **bint** said very quickly, often nearer **bənt**.

The spelling does not change. Only the mouth does.

## 3. Practise

### Task 1 — Which way?

Copy each line and mark with an arrow the direction in which you read it.

1. {{< ar "بَيْت" >}}
2. {{< ar "شارِع ٣٤" >}}
3. `bint`

### Task 2 — Dots

Each row shows the same skeleton. Write the name of the letter.

1. {{< ar "بـ" >}} → ______
2. {{< ar "تـ" >}} → ______
3. {{< ar "ثـ" >}} → ______
4. {{< ar "نـ" >}} → ______
5. {{< ar "يـ" >}} → ______

### Task 3 — Syllables

Read aloud, then write the DIN 31635 transliteration.

{{< ar-block >}}
بَ · بِ · بُ · تَ · تِ · تُ · نَ · نِ · نُ · يَ · يِ · يُ
{{< /ar-block >}}

### Task 4 — Build the word (support ⭑)

Put the letters together, right to left, and read the result.

1. {{< ar "ب" >}} + {{< ar "ـَ" >}} + {{< ar "ي" >}} + {{< ar "ـْ" >}} + {{< ar "ت" >}} → ______
2. {{< ar "ت" >}} + {{< ar "ـِ" >}} + {{< ar "ب" >}} + {{< ar "ـْ" >}} + {{< ar "ن" >}} → ______

### Task 5 — Minimal pairs (extension ⭑⭑)

One dot apart. Read both aloud and say what changes.

1. {{< ar "بَيْت" >}} — {{< ar "تَيْت" >}}
2. {{< ar "بِنْت" >}} — {{< ar "تِنْت" >}}

Only the first word of each pair is a real Arabic word. That is the point: the
dots are not decoration.

{{< details type="tip" title="Answers" >}}

**Task 1.** 1) right to left · 2) the line right to left, but the digits *34*
left to right · 3) left to right (Latin script keeps its own direction).

**Task 2.** 1) bāʾ · 2) tāʾ · 3) ṯāʾ · 4) nūn · 5) yāʾ.

**Task 3.** *ba, bi, bu · ta, ti, tu · na, ni, nu · ya, yi, yu.*

**Task 4.** 1) {{< ar "بَيْت" >}} *bayt* · 2) {{< ar "تِبْن" >}} *tibn*.

**Task 5.** 1) *bayt* → *tayt*: one dot below becomes two above; the word stops
being a word. 2) *bint* → *tint*: the same change. Nothing else about either
word has moved.

{{< /details >}}

## 4. Produce

**Handwriting — your own name.** Using only the five letters of this unit and
the three vowel marks, write as much of your own first name as the letters
allow, right to left, on ruled lines. Where a sound is missing, leave a gap and
mark it with a dash; you will fill the gaps in as the alphabet is completed over
units 2 to 7.

Then write, from memory and without looking back:

1. {{< ar "بَيْت" >}}
2. {{< ar "بِنْت" >}}

**Assessment (self and partner):**

| Criterion | yes | almost | not yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| I wrote from right to left throughout. | | | |
| Every letter has the right number of dots, on the right side. | | | |
| The letters in a word are joined, not spaced apart. | | | |
| I placed each vowel mark on the correct letter. | | | |
| I read both words aloud without hesitating. | | | |

## Notes for teachers

**Timing (45 min):** direction 5' · letter family and dots 12' · vowel marks
10' · tasks 1–3 10' · production 6' · reflection 2'.

**Materials.** Ruled Arabic tracing lines (four-line, with the baseline
emphasised) rather than ordinary exercise paper; the descenders of final *nūn*
and *yāʾ* need the space below the line. Learners should write with the paper
squared to the desk, not tilted as for Latin cursive.

**Differentiation.**
*Support:* restrict the unit to {{< ar "ب" >}}, {{< ar "ت" >}} and
{{< ar "ن" >}}; do task 4 with letter cards laid out on the desk right to left
before any writing.
*Extension:* add {{< ar "ث" >}} and {{< ar "ي" >}} in medial position and have
learners copy {{< ar "تِبْن" >}} and {{< ar "بَيْن" >}} at speed, checking each
other's dots.

**Predictable difficulties.**
- Writing left to right out of habit, then reversing the letters. Catch it in
  the first five minutes; it is very hard to unlearn later.
- Putting the vowel mark on the wrong letter — usually one letter too far left,
  because the eye is still travelling in the Latin direction.
- Reading the *ḍamma* as *o*. It is a short *u*, as in English *put*.
- Treating the joining stroke in a letter table as part of the letter.

**A note on typing.** Discourage typing at this stage. If learners do type,
insist that the vowel marks are typed too; a keyboard that silently drops them
teaches the wrong reading habit from day one.
