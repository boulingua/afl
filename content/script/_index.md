## Why a stage before A1

The CEFR describes what a learner can *do* with a language. It takes for granted
that the learner can read it. For Arabic that assumption fails: a beginner who
has never met the script cannot read a menu, a sign or their own name, and no
amount of spoken practice repairs that. Treating the alphabet as a first-week
warm-up produces learners who decode Arabic through transliteration for years
and never read it fluently.

So the script comes first, as its own stage, with its own units, its own
worksheets and its own audio. It is **not** a CEFR level. Where its can-do
statements map onto the framework's pre-A1 reception and production descriptors
they are recorded as such; where the Companion Volume leaves the cell empty, the
unit records `no-official-descriptor` rather than inventing one.

Three things make Arabic script hard for a first-time reader, and this stage is
organised around all three:

1. **Direction.** Text runs right to left, but numbers inside it run left to
   right, and Latin words embedded in Arabic keep their own direction. Reading
   is a matter of driving the eye deliberately, not of flipping a switch.
2. **Joining.** Letters change shape according to where they stand in the word.
   Most have four forms — initial, medial, final, isolated — and six refuse to
   join to the left at all, which is why {{< ar "دَرَسَ" tr="darasa" >}} has a
   visible gap in the middle and {{< ar "بَنَتْ" tr="banat" >}} does not.
3. **The missing vowels.** Ordinary Arabic writes the consonants and the long
   vowels and leaves the short vowels off. Beginners therefore learn to read
   text that has been *vocalised* — every short vowel written as a small mark
   above or below the letter — and only later learn to do without.

## How to work through it

Write everything by hand. Arabic letterforms are learned in the wrist before
they are learned by the eye, and a learner who has only ever typed Arabic reads
it slowly for years afterwards. Each worksheet has ruled tracing lines for that
reason.

Read aloud, always with the audio. The stage's vocabulary is deliberately tiny —
a few dozen words — because the object of study is the mapping from mark to
sound, not the words themselves. Words you meet here return at A1 with their
meanings attached.

{{< callout type="note" title="Vocalisation in this stage" >}}
Every Arabic string in the script stage and at A1 is **fully vocalised**: each
short vowel is written. From A2 the marks are reduced to those that resolve a
genuine ambiguity, and B1 running text carries none, in line with how Arabic is
printed. Glossary headwords stay vocalised at every level.
{{< /callout >}}

## Numerals

Two sets of digits are in use across the Arab world. The **Eastern Arabic**
numerals {{< ar "٠ ١ ٢ ٣ ٤ ٥ ٦ ٧ ٨ ٩" >}} are standard in the Levant and are
introduced in unit 8; the Western digits 0–9 you already know are standard in
the Maghreb and are what this site's scaffolding prose uses throughout. Note
that both are written left to right even inside a right-to-left line, which is
the first place a reader's eye must learn to change gear.
